A water heater has two slow enemies: sediment and corrosion. Sediment comes from minerals that separate out as water is heated and sink to the bottom of the tank, where they cut efficiency, cause popping and rumbling on gas models, and overheat the steel or bury the lower element on electric ones. Corrosion is held back by the anode rod, a sacrificial rod of magnesium or aluminum that gives itself up so the tank lining is spared. Neither one is visible from the outside, and by the time a homeowner notices, the tank is often leaking. That is the reason maintenance matters for Billings, MT homes: it interrupts both problems while they are still cheap to fix.

Our maintenance visit is built to interrupt both problems. We drain and flush the tank until the water runs clear, pull and inspect the anode rod and recommend a replacement when it is mostly consumed, and test the temperature and pressure relief valve to confirm it opens and reseats. On gas units we inspect the burner, pilot or igniter, thermocouple or flame sensor, flue baffle, and venting for proper draft. On electric units we check element resistance and thermostat operation and look at the wiring for heat damage. We check the expansion tank's air charge against your water pressure, confirm the thermostat setting is near 120 degrees to limit scalding, and look for early leaks at fittings and valves.

Tankless owners need maintenance too, just a different kind. Scale builds up inside the heat exchanger, especially in hard water, and eventually triggers flow problems, fluctuating temperatures, and error codes. We descale the heat exchanger with a pump and descaling solution through the service valves, clean the inlet filter, check the venting and condensate line on condensing models, and review the unit's error history. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • Scale warnings or intermittent shutdowns on a tankless system
  • Hot water running out sooner than it used to
  • No memory of the last time the water heater was serviced
  • A rotten-egg smell from the hot water
  • Energy bills creeping up with no change in hot water use
  • A tank that pops and knocks during a heating cycle
  • Rusty or discolored hot water at the tap
  • Moisture, drips, or rust around the base or fittings

Why Professional Water Heater Maintenance Matters

Some upkeep is fair game for homeowners: watching for leaks, keeping the area around the heater clear, and noting new noises. Beyond that, the work gets riskier. Draining a tank means handling scalding water and a drain valve that may not close again if it is old and full of sediment. Pulling an anode rod often takes a breaker bar and real leverage on a fitting that has not moved in years. Testing a T&P valve on an older heater can leave it dripping afterward. On gas units, the burner, thermocouple, and venting involve combustion safety, and a vent that is not drafting can put carbon monoxide into the house.

We come prepared for what an older heater throws at us: replacement drain valves, anode rods sized for your tank, flexible anodes for tight clearances, T&P valves, and meters for testing elements and thermostats. For tankless systems, we bring the pump and descaling setup. Just as important, we know what to look for, from early corrosion at the fittings to a thermocouple starting to weaken, so you can plan a repair before it becomes a failure. What you get is simple: a heater that lasts longer, runs more efficiently, and does not surprise you with a leak.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Here are our recommendations:

  • Look around the base of the heater periodically for moisture
  • Have the anode rod checked every few years and replaced when it is mostly gone
  • Do not store boxes, paint, or chemicals against the water heater
  • Set the water temperature around 120 degrees for safety and efficiency
  • Stay on top of tankless descaling to avoid error codes and reduced flow
  • Consider a water softener if hard water keeps building sediment and scale
  • Make an annual flush part of your home maintenance routine
